It is the first question of every concrete project and the most expensive one to get wrong: **how many bags do I actually need?**

Order too few and you end up with a half-poured slab — the fresh concrete against the cured concrete will not bond properly, and you get a weak seam called a cold joint. Order too much and you are stuck: mixed concrete cannot go back on the shelf, and most stores will not take returns on opened pallets.

The good news is that the estimate comes down to one formula and one safety margin. Here is the whole method, a chart for common slab sizes, and the point at which bags stop making sense entirely.

## The only formula you need

Concrete bags are sold by weight, but what matters is the **yield** — how much volume one bag produces once mixed. Every bag lists it on the label. The standard yields:

| Bag size | Yield per bag | Bags per cubic yard |
|----------|--------------|---------------------|
| 80 lb | 0.60 cu ft | 45 |
| 60 lb | 0.45 cu ft | 60 |
| 50 lb | 0.375 cu ft | 72 |
| 40 lb | 0.30 cu ft | 90 |

And the volume you are filling, for a rectangular slab or footing:

```
cubic feet = length (ft) × width (ft) × thickness (in) ÷ 12
bags = cubic feet ÷ yield × 1.10   ← 10% for spillage and uneven subgrade
```

The 10% margin is not optional. Subgrade is never perfectly flat, forms flex, and some mix stays in the bag. Do the math without it and you will be making a second trip to the store mid-pour — which is exactly how cold joints happen.

## Bags needed for common slabs (4 inches thick)

Four inches is the standard thickness for patios, walkways and shed slabs. Quick math: 10 × 10 ft × 4 in ÷ 12 = **33.3 cubic feet**. At 0.60 cu ft per 80-lb bag, that is 55.6 bags before waste.

| Slab size | Cubic feet | 80 lb bags | 60 lb bags | 40 lb bags |
|-----------|-----------|------------|------------|------------|
| 4 × 8 | 10.7 | 18 | 24 | 36 |
| 8 × 8 | 21.3 | 36 | 48 | 72 |
| 10 × 10 | 33.3 | 56 | 75 | 112 |
| 12 × 12 | 48.0 | 80 | 107 | 160 |
| 10 × 20 | 66.7 | 112 | 149 | 223 |

(Numbers exclude the 10% waste margin — add it on top.)

## Worked example: a 10×10 patio slab

Let's do it end to end, because seeing the steps is what makes the formula stick:

1. **Volume:** 10 ft × 10 ft × (4 ÷ 12) = 33.3 cu ft (about 1.23 cubic yards)
2. **Bags:** 33.3 ÷ 0.60 = 55.6 → round up to 56 bags of 80 lb
3. **Waste margin:** 56 × 1.10 = 61.6 → **buy 62 bags**

Planning to mix by hand? Sixty-two bags is roughly 5,000 lb of concrete — that is about 2.5 hours of vigorous mixing in a wheelbarrow, and a genuine physical limit. Two people take turns or rent a mixer.

## The fence post shortcut

Post holes are the opposite case — small volumes where people wildly over-buy. A 12-inch diameter hole, 2 feet deep, holds about 1.6 cubic feet. Once you stand a 4×4 post in it, the post displaces most of a bag's worth of volume, so:

- **A 12" × 24" hole with a 4×4 post needs about two 80-lb bags**, three to be safe if the hole is wider or deeper than planned.

The trap here is diameter: a 16-inch hole holds nearly twice as much as a 12-inch one. If you are setting a whole run of fence posts, a couple of inches of over-digging per hole adds up to full extra pallets. ## When to stop buying bags

Bagged concrete has a hard economic ceiling. As a rule of thumb:

- **Under ~1 cubic yard** (27 bags of 80 lb equivalent): bags make sense
- **1 to 1.5 cubic yards**: a grey zone — compare prices honestly, including your back
- **Over 1.5 cubic yards**: order a ready-mix truck

Two reasons the truck wins at scale. First, price per volume: an 80-lb bag at around $7 works out to roughly $11.70 per cubic foot, while ready-mix typically runs $150–$200 per cubic yard — about $5.50–$7.40 per cubic foot. Second, and just as important: a truck pours 1.2 cubic yards in minutes, continuously. Hand-mixing that volume means the first batch is setting up while you are still mixing the middle — a quality problem, not just a labor problem. ## The gravel version of the same problem

Gravel is sold by the ton, which adds one more conversion. Say you are resurfacing a 12 × 50 ft driveway at 4 inches deep:

1. **Volume:** 12 × 50 × (4 ÷ 12) = 200 cu ft = 7.4 cubic yards
2. **Tons:** gravel runs roughly 1.4 tons per cubic yard → **10 to 11 tons**
3. **Depth matters more than people think:** for vehicles, a driveway wants 4–6 inches of compacted gravel — skimp on depth and it ruts within a season

The [gravel driveway calculator](https://www.calcforhome.com/gravel-driveway-calculator/) converts area and depth into cubic yards and tons, and the [gravel driveway depth guide](https://www.calcforhome.com/gravel-driveway-depth-calculator/) covers how deep to go for cars versus heavy trucks.

## Mistakes that cost real money

- **Forgetting to convert inches.** Thickness entered as "4" instead of 4/12 of a foot inflates the estimate 3×. Always divide inches by 12 first.
- **Using mortar or sand mix yields.** Mortar mix, sand mix and fast-setting post mix have different yields than standard concrete mix. Check the yield on the actual bag you are buying, not a generic chart.
- **Skipping the waste margin.** 10% on a 30-bag job is 3 bags — far cheaper than a cold joint.
- **Ignoring bag size economics.** The 80-lb bag is almost always the cheapest per cubic foot (about 28% cheaper than the 40-lb). Buy fewer heavy bags unless there is a carrying problem.

One more tip before you pour: the coverage numbers assume the bag was stored dry. Bags that have absorbed moisture lose yield and strength, so if the pallet at the store looks weathered, pick a different store rather than a different number.
